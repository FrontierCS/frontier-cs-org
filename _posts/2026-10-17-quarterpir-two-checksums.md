In its [FrontierCS 2]({{ '/#leaderboard' | relative_url }}) run on the QuarterPIR task, Muse Wonder 1.0 cut the bytes of each private lookup by 27%, with privacy unchanged. The fix is a trick from RAID-6 disk arrays: two checksums can rebuild two lost values. Every model on the leaderboard ran the task once, and seven of twelve passed. No other passing run saved more than 96 of the 1,760 bytes per lookup. Muse Wonder 1.0 saved 480.

## The Task

FrontierCS 2 removes the key part of a paper's released code, and an agent writes it back. The authors' code is the human reference. A run passes only if its final version beats it on every metric of every hidden workload.

QuarterPIR, a EUROCRYPT 2024 scheme by Ghoshal, Zhou and Shi [1], lets a client read one database entry without the server learning which. The [task]({{ '/task/#quarterpir' | relative_url }}) removes the server's request handler, and the agent may redesign both messages. A pass needs fewer bytes on every lookup, faster times and no more client memory.

## The Scheme on 16 Entries

The client reads the database once and keeps hints. A hint picks one entry per row, such as B, G, I and O, and the client stores only their XOR, the parity $$h$$. To read G, it asks for the parity of B, F, I and O, with G swapped for a spare entry F whose value it knows. XORing the reply with $$h$$ leaves G ⊕ F, and one more XOR gives G.

## Why Every Reply Carries the Key Parities

A hint serves one lookup. After reading G, a backup hint for row 2 takes the place of the used one, with its entry in row 2 swapped for G. Ghoshal, Zhou and Shi [1] show that this keeps the table of hints distributed as before.

The keys of the new hint still pick H, not G. When the hint is used later, the key drawn to pick G picks other entries too, and the client needs the server's key parity for that group. Asking for one would reveal where the last read lay, so every reply carries all of them.

## The Two Checksums

Muse Wonder 1.0 sends two sums of the key parities instead of all of them. The key parities of the server and of the client agree in every group but two: the group of the wanted entry and the group of the inserted one. The client knows both groups. A plain sum and a weighted sum give it two equations, and it solves for the difference it needs.

## Why Privacy Is Unchanged
